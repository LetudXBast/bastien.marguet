Who am I?

I am a PhD in statistical physics, and I work a lot on how to make complex ideas understandable. I won the regional final of Ma Thèse en 180 secondes, where researchers explain their work in just three minutes.

Since then, I have spoken in different formats — podcasts, TV, public events — and I now train students in public speaking and eloquence.

Why am I here with you?

Your future work is not only about language. It is also about meaning, clarity, and real-time understanding.

Today, we will work on a simple question:

How do we make an idea understandable?

Opening question

Have you ever explained something… and realized the other person didn’t understand?

For example:

  • explaining your studies or your future job
  • defending an idea in a debate
  • talking about politics, the environment, AI, language…
  • or simply explaining something obvious to you

Think about one real situation

  • What were you trying to explain?
  • Who were you talking to?
  • What did you notice on their side?
  • At what exact moment did you realize they did not understand?

Core idea

Having the words is not enough.

Explaining is not the same as being understood.

Why this matters for interpreters

  • You do not only transfer words
  • You reconstruct meaning
  • You work under time pressure
  • You make ideas accessible for a real audience

Today’s workshop

  1. Structure ideas clearly
  2. Adapt to the audience
  3. Use metaphor carefully
  4. Practice speaking with feedback
  5. See how communication changes everything

Exercise 1 — Make me care

You have 60 seconds.

Introduce yourself — but do not just list facts.

Make me interested.

Exercise 1 — Structure

  1. Who you are
  2. What you study / work on
  3. Why it matters

Round 1 → feedback → Round 2 → feedback → Round 3

What to listen for

  • Was it clear?
  • Did I understand what this person studies?
  • Did I understand why I should care?
  • At what moment did I lose attention?

What usually goes wrong

  • Too many details
  • No clear point
  • No connection with the listener
  • No ending

A simple tool

1. What is it?

2. How does it work?

3. Why does it matter?

Why the third sentence matters

  • If there is no stake, attention drops
  • The listener needs a reason to stay with you
  • Relevance supports understanding

Exercise 2 — Explain a concept in 3 sentences

Choose one:

  • What is translation?
  • What is interpretation?
  • What is ambiguity?
  • What is irony?

  • What is fidelity in translation?

Exercise 2 — Constraints

  • 3 sentences maximum
  • No jargon
  • Understandable by a non-expert
  • Clear beginning, clear end

Change the audience

Now explain the same idea to:

  • a 12-year-old
  • your grandparents
  • someone outside your field

What changed?

  • Vocabulary
  • Sentence length
  • Level of abstraction
  • Examples

Clarity is selection

To explain clearly is not to say everything.

Metaphor

  • A metaphor can make an abstract idea concrete
  • It helps create a mental image
  • But it can also distort meaning

Exercise 3 — Add one metaphor

Take your previous explanation.

Add one metaphor to make it easier to understand.

Test your metaphor

  • What does it help the listener understand?
  • What does it hide?
  • At what point can it become misleading?

A good metaphor

  • clarifies one mechanism
  • creates a useful image
  • does not produce false understanding

My example

A concept from my PhD in statistical physics

First: expert version

Version 1 — Expert language

In statistical physics, we often study how collective macroscopic behaviors emerge from microscopic interactions between a very large number of particles. The challenge is that even when the local rules are simple, the global dynamics can become highly non-trivial, especially near phase transitions or in non-equilibrium systems.

What happened when you heard that?

  • What did you understand?
  • What did you not understand?
  • What blocked you?

Version 2 — Public explanation

My research looks at a simple question: how can many small individual actions create a large collective behavior?

It is a bit like a crowd: each person moves locally, but together they can suddenly create order, chaos, or a traffic jam.

Understanding these collective effects helps us think about many systems, from materials to living organisms and social dynamics.

What changed?

  • Less jargon
  • Clearer structure
  • More concrete imagery
  • A visible stake
  • A more accessible rhythm

Communication changes everything

The idea is not the same if the audience cannot enter it.

Exercise 4 — Under pressure

I give you a short explanation.

You have 1 minute to understand it.

Then you explain it clearly in 30–45 seconds.

What to focus on

  • Identify the core idea
  • Keep the logic
  • Remove what is unnecessary
  • Preserve meaning

Body and voice

  • Slow down
  • Use pauses
  • Articulate
  • Look at the audience
  • Let key ideas land

Delivery supports clarity. It does not replace it.

Key ideas to remember

  • Understanding is not automatic
  • Clarity is built
  • Structure creates comprehension
  • Good communication requires choices

Final message

If people do not understand, the message failed.

Thank you
